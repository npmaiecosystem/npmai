Living Under the Line
A comparative field survey of Hindu minority communities across South Asia

Fifteen thousand three hundred and forty household interviews, gathered street by street across twelve regions in Pakistan, Bangladesh, India and Nepal, on what it feels like to hold a minority faith where you live. This report reads that testimony against what independent monitors have already documented, and asks what holds the difference between the four countries in place.

Executive Summary

Four countries, one instrument, four very different answers

Every respondent answered the same twenty five questions. What separates the four national profiles below is not the survey design, it is what each state and society does with a minority in its midst.

Finding 01

Security is bimodal, not a spectrum

94.7% of respondents in Pakistan and Bangladesh describe themselves as "not secure" or "extremely insecure," against 96.9% of Nepali respondents who say the opposite, "fully secure." There is almost no shared middle ground between the two poles.

Finding 02

The state is where the pattern breaks

Only 4.9% of complaints filed in Pakistan and Bangladesh led to any action, against 40.3% in India and 81.2% in Nepal. The gap in outcomes is larger than the gap in what people report experiencing.

Finding 03

Conversion pressure is concentrated, not general

44.6% of Pakistani and Bangladeshi respondents report direct pressure or threat to convert; India and Nepal report none of that category at all. This is the sharpest single divide in the entire dataset.

Finding 04

Perceived trajectory tracks institutional trust, not just incidents

75.2% of respondents in Pakistan and Bangladesh believe their situation has worsened over the last five to ten years, closely tracking how little faith they hold in police and administration, more than it tracks any single category of incident.

01

Introduction

Most of what the world knows about the treatment of Hindu minorities in South Asia comes from incident counts: a temple vandalised, a girl abducted, a family displaced, tallied by human rights commissions and press monitors into annual reports. That documentation is essential, and this report leans on it throughout. But an incident count cannot tell you what it is like to wake up every day inside that statistic, whether the fear is constant or occasional, whether people still send their daughters to school, whether anyone still bothers calling the police.

This report tries to close that gap. Between the two documentation layers, incident-level reporting from established monitors, and household-level lived experience from the communities themselves, sits a more complete picture than either layer offers alone. NPMAI Social Foundation's Research Political Science department coordinated a twenty five question household survey across identified Hindu-population localities in Pakistan, Bangladesh, India and Nepal, run through the Foundation's regional volunteer network over roughly seven weeks.

The three questions this report tries to answer are simple to state and harder to answer honestly: what do households in these communities report about their own safety, treatment, and prospects; how consistent is that report across a Muslim-majority-state bloc versus India and Nepal; and where the report lines up with, or fills a gap in, what independent monitors have already found.

02

Methodology

This was a targeted, volunteer-administered field survey, not a probability sample, and every figure in this report should be read as describing the localities surveyed, not the national population of any country.

Design

Paper-form, street-intercept survey administered by NPMAI Social Foundation's regional volunteer network in identified Hindu-population localities. Respondents ticked their own answers on a printed form; volunteers did not read questions aloud or prompt responses.

Window

Fieldwork ran approximately 1.7 months, coordinated in stages: preliminary desk review of existing news and human rights reporting, a planning session with the regional technical team, then simultaneous field collection across all twelve regions.

Sample by Region

Data cleaning and exclusions

The compiled dataset presented in this report reflects a cleaned analytic sample. The research team excluded, prior to analysis: forms left substantially incomplete (respondents who answered some sections and abandoned others, for example giving a location but skipping occupation and education questions), and forms flagged by regional coordinators as filled by respondents who did not meet the Hindu-respondent criterion the survey was targeting. This exclusion step was applied by the coordinating team rather than through an automated, independently audited process, which is a limitation this report treats explicitly in Section 09, not as a footnote.

On scope of claims. Pakistan and Bangladesh responses are statistically close to one another across nearly every question in this instrument. Rather than overstate a country-by-country contrast the underlying numbers do not clearly support, this report treats Pakistan and Bangladesh as a single comparative bloc, and reserves country-specific claims for places where independently reported, named incidents support them directly.

Instrument

Twenty five questions across eleven thematic blocks, ranging from demographic context through sense of security, documented incidents, education and health access, government relations, majority-community relations, food security, fear typology, conversion pressure, and perceived trajectory. The full instrument is reproduced in the Appendix.

05

Comparative Synthesis

To compare the three blocs on one scale, this report builds a simple composite score from three of the clearest indicators: reported security (Q6), trust that a complaint leads to action (Q10), and freedom from social boycott (Q18). The formula is shown openly below so the score can be checked or challenged.

Vulnerability Index = 100 minus the average of: (percent reporting "not secure" or "extremely insecure") , (percent whose complaint led to no action or was never filed out of fear or distrust) , (percent reporting boycott "frequently" or "occasionally")

Pakistan + Bangladesh
Low security score, driven by state non-response and high boycott frequency
India (surveyed districts)
Mid-range, institutional responsiveness offsets moderate social friction
Nepal
Majority-Hindu state, used here as an upper-bound reference point

Read this index as descriptive, not diagnostic. It compresses eleven survey themes into three; the fuller picture sits in Section 04. What it makes visible at a glance is that the gap between blocs is driven less by the frequency of any single bad event, and more by whether an institution exists that a household believes will respond to one.

External Triangulation

This survey does not stand alone. The themes it surfaces at household level align closely with what independent human rights monitors, UN experts, and national rights commissions have already documented at incident level over the same period.

The alignment across these four rows is the core evidentiary claim of this report: household-level testimony, gathered independently of any single news cycle, points in the same direction as incident-level monitoring built from FIRs, press reports and commission fieldwork. Neither source proves the other, but together they are considerably harder to dismiss than either alone.

07

Structural Discussion

The explanatory question this report asks is not who believes what, but which structures allow belief to convert into consequence. Three mechanisms recur across both this survey and the independent documentation cited above, and none of them require characterising an entire religious population to explain.

Institutional non-response

The single clearest divide in this dataset is not what happens to people, it is what happens after. A complaint filed in Nepal resolves 81.2% of the time; the same act in the Pakistan and Bangladesh bloc resolves 4.9% of the time. That gap is a policing and judicial outcome, not a demographic one.

Legal instruments open to misuse

Blasphemy law provisions in Pakistan and property statutes such as Bangladesh's Vested Property Act are named repeatedly in HRCP and USCIRF reporting as mechanisms through which land and false-case pressure are applied to minority households. These are specific, identifiable legal instruments, not a claim about belief.

Named organised actors

Where independent monitors attribute specific incidents to organised political or religious groups, this report follows that attribution rather than generalising. Accountability sits with identifiable actors and the state responses that fail to check them.

Majoritarian nationalism as a comparative frame

Political science literature on states that tie national identity to a single religious majority offers a structural explanation for why minority protections erode under majoritarian pressure, a pattern documented across many countries and religious majorities, not unique to any one of them.

Read together, the argument this report supports is that where state institutions decline to enforce equal protection, whether through inaction, permissive legal instruments, or tolerance of organised non-state actors, minority communities absorb the cost regardless of their own conduct. That is a claim about governance, and it is the claim this survey's own numbers actually sustain.

Recommendations

National Governments

Mandatory FIR registration timelines for minority-linked complaints, independent audit of blasphemy and vested-property law application, and published quarterly minority-incident data from police services.

International Bodies

Continued OHCHR and USCIRF monitoring cadence, and conditioning of relevant development or trade engagement on measurable improvement in complaint-resolution rates, not incident counts alone.

NPMAI Social Foundation

We Commit ourselves that in future we will increase sample size and also ensure better quality data and digitalise the survey process.

09

Limitations

This section is written to be read, not skipped. A report on a subject this serious earns credibility by stating plainly what it cannot show.

  • Non-probability sample. Respondents were reached through targeted street-intercept collection in identified Hindu-majority localities, not random selection. Findings describe those localities, not national populations.
  • Pakistan and Bangladesh are not independently distinguishable in this data. Response distributions across the two countries are close enough that this report treats them as one comparative bloc rather than asserting country-specific differences the data cannot support.
  • No independent verification of individual incidents. Every incident figure in this report is self-reported prevalence, not a verified case count. It should be read as "share of respondents who report experiencing," not as a confirmed tally of events.
  • Manual exclusion process. Incomplete and flagged-as-non-target responses were removed by the coordinating research team rather than through an independently audited automated process. Future waves should formalise this step with a documented, reviewable protocol.
  • Flat subgroup variation. Age, gender and occupation subgroups show little internal variation on outcome questions within a country in this dataset. This report does not draw gender- or age-specific conclusions from the survey; that evidence would need to come from targeted qualitative follow-up, not this instrument.
  • Small Nepal sample relative to the other three countries. Nepal's 800 respondents, reached through a comparatively thin volunteer network, should be read as an indicative reference point rather than a fully powered national estimate.
  • Recall and framing bias. Self-reported feelings of security and recollection of incidents over a five year window are subject to ordinary recall bias and the psychological effect of having recently relocated to a safer area, which this report discusses directly in Section 04.1.
10

Conclusion

Fifteen thousand three hundred and forty conversations, conducted on the ground rather than compiled from a wire report, describe two very different regions occupying the same subcontinent. In Pakistan and Bangladesh, a household's safety, its access to schooling and healthcare, and its standing before the law all move together, and they move in the same direction as the incident counts that international monitors have separately documented over the same period. In India's surveyed districts and in Nepal, the same questions produce a materially different answer, not because the underlying communities differ, but because the institutions around them do.

That is the finding this report is prepared to defend: not a claim about a people, but a measurable claim about what happens, and does not happen, when a state declines to protect a minority inside it. NPMAI Social Foundation intends to repeat this survey on a fixed interval, publish the underlying data for independent scrutiny, and track whether that gap closes or widens.
